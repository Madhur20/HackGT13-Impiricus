Your instinct here is correct, and I'd trust it over the filter approach. Let me explain why precisely, then build out the categorical design in detail.

Why categorical wins — the actual risk-model difference
A pre-send filter is a detection system: it has to correctly recognize every way a patient detail could be phrased, which means it has a false-negative rate by definition — there will always be some phrasing, some edge case, some clever rewording that slips through, because natural language is infinite and your filter's training data isn't. That's not a solvable problem, it's a fundamental property of detecting things in free text.

Categorical input is a prevention system: if the only things a doctor can select are "GLP-1 receptor agonists," "renal impairment, stage 3–4," and "adult, 40–65," there is no text field for a patient's name, date, or specific story to go into — the vocabulary of what can be said doesn't include patient-identifying content at all. You're not catching bad input, you're removing the possibility of it existing in the first place. That's a structural guarantee, not a probabilistic one, and it's the only version of this you could honestly describe to Impiricus's legal team as close to airtight.

My recommendation: categorical as the core architecture, full stop. Keep a filter only as a thin second layer on the one small free-text surface I'll describe below — not because the filter is your main defense, but because defense-in-depth is good practice even when your primary control is strong.

The categorical workflow, concretely
Step 1 — Drug/therapeutic area. Searchable dropdown, pulled from formulary data you already have (e.g., "GLP-1 receptor agonists," "SGLT2 inhibitors," "biologics — TNF inhibitors").

Step 2 — Question type. Fixed list: Dosing/titration · Side-effect management · Drug interaction · Switching therapy · Monitoring protocol · Administration/formulation.

Step 3 — Population bracket. Age range, not exact age: Pediatric · Adult 18–40 · Adult 40–65 · Geriatric 65+.

Step 4 — Relevant comorbidity tags (multi-select, not free text). A checklist: Renal impairment · Hepatic impairment · Pregnancy · Cardiac disease · Diabetes · None/other.

Step 5 — The system assembles the question for the doctor, from their selections — they never type it themselves. For example, selecting GLP-1 / dosing / adult 40–65 / renal impairment auto-generates:

"How do peers approach GLP-1 titration in adult patients (40–65) with stage 3–4 renal impairment?"

The doctor reviews and confirms this generated sentence rather than writing their own — this is the detail that matters most: removing free-text authorship removes the risk vector entirely, rather than hoping a filter catches what they typed.

Step 6 — Matching. Same profile-similarity engine as Practice Mirror, now matching on the structured tags instead of prose.

Step 7 — Response. The matched peer answers using the same structured-first approach — pre-set response patterns ("Typical approach: start at [dose], titrate over [timeframe], monitor for [category]") with a very short, capped free-text field (say, 200 characters) for a clarifying nuance, which is the one place I'd still run a lightweight filter, precisely because it's the only place free text exists at all.

Is this actually useful, or just safe?
I think it's genuinely useful, and there's a good reason why: this is close to how experienced physicians already frame curbside consults, because they're already trained not to share identifying details with colleagues informally. "How do you approach GLP-1 dosing in renal-impaired patients" is a completely normal, real question a doctor would ask a colleague at a conference — you're not distorting the interaction, you're just making its natural shape the only shape available.

There's a bonus you didn't ask for but is worth knowing: because every question is structured, every answer becomes a reusable, searchable knowledge artifact — the next doctor with a similar drug/population/comorbidity combination can find a prior answer instead of needing a live match at all. Free-text chat can't do this safely (you can't index and resurface raw PHI-adjacent conversation), but a categorical Q&A pair can become a genuine, growing knowledge base over time — which is a real product moat, not just a compliance workaround.

The honest limitation: some genuinely unusual, multi-comorbidity, edge-case clinical situations won't fit cleanly into fixed categories. Two mitigations: allow multiple comorbidity tags per question (already in the design above), and add a governed "suggest a new category" path — if enough doctors hit "other" for the same underlying thing, Impiricus's own team reviews and adds it as a real category later. That's a controlled expansion process, not an open door.

Moving off-platform — how I'd design it, and why it's actually a smart liability move, not a loophole
This is a well-established pattern (LinkedIn, professional referral networks, even dating apps): the platform's job is the introduction; what happens after is between two consenting adults on their own tools. You can lean into that deliberately:

After a structured Q&A exchange (or several), either doctor can tap "Continue this conversation."
This triggers a mutual, explicit opt-in — both doctors must independently confirm before either party's contact info (email, phone, whatever they choose to share) is revealed. Neither side sees anything unless both say yes — same double-opt-in pattern as the apps above.
Before the exchange completes, both doctors see a clear, unambiguous disclosure: "Conversations outside Impiricus are not monitored, logged, or moderated by Impiricus. As licensed clinicians, your professional and regulatory obligations continue to apply to any discussion you have independently."
Impiricus logs that an exchange happened (timestamp, which two NPIs, nothing else) for audit purposes, but never touches the content of whatever happens afterward — because it isn't happening on their platform anymore.
This is a genuinely strong legal position, not just a way to dodge responsibility: Impiricus's liability exposure is tied to what happens on their platform, and once two independently-licensed physicians choose to text or call each other on their own phones, that's squarely their own professional conduct — the same as if they'd met at a medical conference and swapped business cards. You're not building a chat feature and hoping it's fine; you're deliberately keeping the highest-risk, least-structured part of the interaction off Impiricus's infrastructure entirely, by design.

One framing note for the pitch: don't market the off-platform step as "a way to have the real conversation" — that undercuts the whole categorical design by implying the structured version is a lesser, fallback experience. Frame it instead as "for doctors who want an ongoing collegial relationship beyond a single question" — a natural next step for a good match, not an escape hatch from the safe version.
